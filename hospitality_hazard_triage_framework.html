Natural Disaster, Fire, and Hazard Triage Framework for Hospitality Venues

Purpose: This framework helps a hospitality emergency AI rank real-time incidents such as fires, floods, landslides, earthquakes, gas leaks, and similar venue hazards without blindly trusting the hazard label. It combines a venue's baseline hazard exposure with live incident severity so that, for example, a small contained fire can rank below an extreme flood if the actual danger is higher.

1. Core Principle

The AI should not rank incidents by hazard type alone. Use a two-layer model:

Final Priority = (Base Hazard Prior x 0.20) + (Live Incident Score x 0.80)
Live Incident Score = 0.30V + 0.20S + 0.20I + 0.15H + 0.10L + 0.05P

Where:

  • Base Hazard Prior = site-specific preparedness risk for that venue and season, 0-10
  • V = Venue life-safety threat, 0-10
  • S = Severity and spread, 0-10
  • I = Immediate intervention need, 0-10
  • H = Hazard context and site amplifiers, 0-10
  • L = Location, access, and evacuation difficulty, 0-10
  • P = Propagation or multi-person impact, 0-10
Why this matters: A small contained kitchen fire should not automatically outrank a fast-rising occupied flood event. The live incident score should dominate the final rank.

4. Base Hazard Prior

Base Hazard Prior is a venue-specific score, not a universal score. A coastal resort in monsoon season should have a higher flood prior than an inland city hotel. A hill resort in heavy rain should have a higher landslide prior than a business hotel in a flat urban area.

Suggested inputs:

  • Property geography
  • Season and forecast
  • Building type and height
  • Historical incidents
  • Occupancy profile
  • Known infrastructure weaknesses

Examples:

  • Fire in dense high-rise city hotel: 8.0
  • Flood at coastal or riverside property in monsoon: 7.5-9.0
  • Landslide at hill resort during heavy rain: 8.0-9.0
  • Earthquake at a zone IV or V property: 6.5-8.5

V = min(10, A + E + C + O)

  • E = Escalation speed, 0-3
  • C = Critical systems compromise, 0-2
  • O = Occupants in immediate danger, 0-3

6.3 I - Immediate Intervention Need

I = min(10, Li + Ri + Ci)
  • Li = Lifesaving action need, 0-6
  • Ri = Risk if delayed, 0-3
  • Ci = Complexity or response level, 0-2

  • +3 rescue or extraction of trapped occupants needed

  • +2 immediate shutdown needed, such as gas, power, or water isolation
  • +1 suppression or smoke control action needed now
  • +1 immediate medical support needed for exposed guests or staff

7. Hard Override Rules

The formula is useful, but safety systems also need deterministic minimum-score rules.

  • Smoke in an escape corridor - minimum score 8.5
  • Water above knee level and rising in occupied zone - minimum score 8.5
  • Structural collapse, active landslide impact, or major gas leak - minimum score 9.0
  • Confirmed trapped occupants - minimum score 9.0
  • Multiple exits blocked on an occupied floor - minimum score 8.5
  • Contained event with no spread and no occupant exposure - cap near 4.5 unless worsening

8. How the AI Becomes Aware

The system should fuse signals from multiple sources:

  • Building systems: smoke detectors, heat sensors, sprinkler flow, water level sensors, gas detectors, structural tilt or vibration, elevator and door status, power and backup status
  • External feeds: weather alerts, flood alerts, seismic alerts, storm advisories, road closures
  • Occupancy and context: checked-in rooms, banquet attendance, staff locations, special-needs flags, floor plans, exit maps
  • Guest and staff input: SOS buttons, panic buttons, emergency extension, app reports, staff confirmations
  • Vision and audio intelligence: smoke visibility, crowding, debris, water accumulation, distress sounds, blocked corridor detection
  • Network health: internet status, cellular quality, local Wi-Fi and mesh availability, UPS and edge server status

9. Worked Example: Occupied-Floor Fire in a Hotel Tower

9.1 Incident Awareness

A fire starts in a service pantry on Floor 12 of a city hotel at 9:18 PM.

Signals received:

  • Smoke detector alarm on Floor 12
  • Sprinkler flow in pantry
  • Corridor camera detects falling visibility
  • Staff app confirms smell of smoke
  • One stairwell door sensor shows heavy use and congestion
  • PMS shows 73 guests and 12 staff in the affected stack
  • Internet is down, but the hotel LAN and local emergency hub are still alive

9.3 Factor Scoring

  • V = 8. Active smoke in occupied corridor, worsening over minutes, one egress path compromised, occupants in direct exposure zone.
  • S = 7. Spread beyond origin room into corridor, visibility deteriorating, one floor stack affected and growing.
  • I = 8. Partial floor evacuation needed now, emergency team needed immediately, delay increases entrapment risk.
  • H = 6. High-rise occupied hotel, nighttime occupancy, internet outage increases coordination dependency.
  • L = 7. Upper-middle floors, one route degraded, elevators should not be used normally.
  • P = 6. Dozens of people in affected stack, panic possible, HVAC and corridor spread possible.

9.4 Score Calculation

Live Incident Score = 0.30(8) + 0.20(7) + 0.20(8) + 0.15(6) + 0.10(7) + 0.05(6)
Live Incident Score = 2.4 + 1.4 + 1.6 + 0.9 + 0.7 + 0.3 = 7.3

Assume Base Hazard Prior for fire at this high-rise hotel is 8.0.

Final Priority = 0.20(8.0) + 0.80(7.3) = 1.6 + 5.84 = 7.44

But a hard override applies:

  • Smoke in an escape corridor - minimum 8.5

Final Priority = 8.5 / 10
Tier = Extreme
Color = Red

10. Immediate Orchestration Steps

  1. Alerting: notify security, engineering, floor wardens, medic team, and external responders if configured.
  2. Guest communication: send offline alerts to devices on Floors 11-13 with color-coded floor-plan evacuation routes and blocked paths.
  3. Mesh / WebRTC mode: if internet is down, shift to local signaling and local mesh to preserve room-to-command and staff-to-staff communication.
  4. Nearest-aid routing: route nearest staff to extinguishers, hose reels, stair doors, AEDs, first-aid points, and room-check duties.
  5. Building controls: disable guest elevator use, prioritize emergency movement, manage smoke doors if configured, preserve power to emergency systems.
  6. Continuous re-ranking: recompute every 15-30 seconds. Downgrade if suppression succeeds and the corridor clears. Escalate if trapped guests are detected.

11. Small Fire vs Extreme Flood Sanity Check

Scenario Base Prior Live Score Final Priority Tier Why
Small pantry fire, contained, no smoke spread, no guest exposure 8.0 2.8 3.8 Medium Fire label has a high baseline, but the incident is controlled
Basement or lobby flood, water rising fast, exits blocked, occupied zone 7.5 8.8 8.5 Extreme Flood baseline is lower, but the real-time danger is much higher

12. Reporting Format

Every incident should be reported with a score, explanation, and action.

Incident: Fire
Location: Floor 12 Service Pantry and Corridor
Priority: 8.5/10
Tier: Extreme
Confidence: 0.88

Why ranked high:
- Smoke detected in occupied escape corridor
- 85 people in affected floor stack
- One stairwell congested or degraded
- Event spreading beyond origin point
- Internet unavailable, local mesh active

Immediate actions:
- Push red evacuation popup to Floors 11-13
- Route guests to Stairwell A only
- Dispatch fire team, security, and medic team
- Disable guest elevator access
- Alert fire brigade and ambulance
- Recompute in 20 seconds

13. Recommended Product Behavior

  • Use deterministic rules for thresholds, overrides, evacuation logic, and responder escalation.
  • Use AI for summarization, ranking assistance, and natural-language reporting.
  • Show current priority score, tier, confidence, top reasons, next actions, and time since last update.
  • Re-rank all active incidents continuously rather than relying on one-time labels.

Summary: The right system does not ask, "Is fire usually more dangerous than flood?" It asks, "Which incident is most dangerous right now at this venue, to these people, given current conditions and access constraints?"
